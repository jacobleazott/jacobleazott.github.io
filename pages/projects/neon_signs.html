Neon Signs

Overview

You have most certainly come across custom LED neon signs before. At least when I first began creating these it seemed most were simply attached to a piece of acrylic, acceptable from a distance but showed wiring and plastic housing up close. Why not fully 3D print the enclosure? It would provide a cleaner look and be far more durable. Over time I developed a streamlined process to go from a blank canvas to a fully finished sign in under a day (depending on size and complexity of course). I have since made frankly way too many signs, not only for myself but for friends and family.

How to Create Your Own Neon Sign

This step-by-step guide will walk you through building your own customizable Langton's Ant simulator.

Step 0: Tools and Materials

There are a few different ways to create your own sign and there is plenty of room for differing tools and materials depending on what is available to you. However, the following are what I use.

Materials

  • LED 'Neon' Rope (color(s) of your choice)
  • 3D Printer Filament (I use PLA)
  • 12V DC Power Supply
  • DC Barrel Plugs or Cables
  • Wire
  • Solder
  • Hot Glue (optional)
  • Liquid Electrical Tape (optional)

Tools

  • 3D Modeling Software
  • Vector Graphics Software
  • 3D Printer
  • Soldering Iron
  • Wire Strippers
  • Hot Glue Gun (optional)

Step 1: Design

  1. Usually the most difficult part of this process is coming up with a design that you like and that is reasonable. The decisions you make in this step will determine the time needed and overall complexity of wiring your sign together.
  2. In your vector graphics software of choice draw out your sign treating single lines as segments of the sign. Set your stroke width for that line to 9.5mm. This allows for 2mm walls on either side of the 5.5mm wide LED 'rope'. If you are using different sized LEDs or want thinner/ thicker walls you should make that decision now.
  3. An important note here is to make sure any curves or corners have at least a 3mm radius so that the LED rope can bend correctly.
  4. Now most LED neon rope is cuttable in 25mm segments. This is crucial in the design step as you will want to make sure your vector segments are close to that size. Now this is where you will need to simply have some experience to know what are acceptable tolerances for the lengths. There are many ways to fix this in the wiring stage but from personal experience I have found that shooting for around +/- 10-15 mm outside of the 25mm segments is usually good enough. This is because depending on curves and if you lay the LEDs on the inner or outer radius you will have differeing lengths. You also need to make sure to have room for having a soldered connection to the end of each segment. For purely straight segments you can get a lot closer to the 25mm lengh but make sure you are always overshooting a bit. It is a lot easier to put the LED segments into 30mm segments than 24mm segments.
  5. In Adobe Illustrator it is very easy to get this information. Just open the 'Document Info' tab, select 'Objects', then select your singular path. It will display the length under 'Paths'. If you are too close to a 25mm increment consider shortening/ lengthening the segment or playing around with the curves.
  6. Once you have your design finallized it is time to export it as an SVG for our 3D modeling software.

Step 2: 3D Modeling and Printing

  1. Once your svg is imported into your 3D modeling software go ahead and extrude it 14mm . This will be our 'base' object for our sign.
  2. Next we need to "shell" the object with 2mm walls leaving the top side open. This creates the channel for the LED rope.
  3. Now we need to make the cable management pathways by extruding the shelled walls -17mm from the top. Making sure to of course join the extrusion and not cut. This creates a 3mm recess for the wires to fit into.
  4. Next you should connect any "floating" segments or segments that will need more support so the sign holds its shape as well as allows a path for all of the wires. I only extrude these "connectors" 10mm high and typically 9.5mm wide to stay consistent with the rest of the sign. Make sure to also have the "channel" cut into the bottom of these segments to allow for the wires to go through.
  5. Finally make some 5mm wide holes at the end of each segment so the wires can pass through and connect each LED segment. You can leave one segments end closed if you know that will be the last segment.
  6. Completely optional but you can also put a cutout in the bottom wire channel to accomodate your DC cable. However, I usually make this decision after it is printed and just melt it down with a soldering iron.
  7. You are now ready to print! At this point you can cut the sign up into pieces if it is too big for your printer. I usually try to cut it at the connecting segments but there is really no difference since it would be very hard to see the seam from a distance. For printing settings it really doesn't matter. All of the overhangs are just small 5.5mm segments and if you have some sagging it doesn't matter since this is just for the wires. I typically print on full draft settings (lowest quality) but do add a brim especially if you cut the sign up to better support those small 2mm wall corners.

Step 3: Wiring

  1. This is the final stretch of the project but can be quite tedious and time consuming. The first step is to make sure your LED segments fit snugly into the channel and all segments will fit the 25mm segment cut points. I typically do this just by taking the end of my LED rope and pushing it in until it reaches the end, if it doesn't look like it will fit well go ahead and flip the segment around since you will get a different length due to the curves. This is important to do now as if you have made a mistake you don't want to find out halfway through wiring the sign.
  2. Once you have verified all the segments will work it is time to start soldering leads onto the LEDs. Most LED ropes I have come across are not direction but make sure to verify this before you start. I first start cut the LED segment to length. A tip if you aren't too confident in your soldering ability is to "sacrifice" one 25mm segment by cutting far into the next segment so you have more pad to work with, then doing this to the start of the next segment so both ends of your pads have plenty space to solder.
  3. Next I take an exacto and cut away the plastic housing from the end of the LED segment to get easy access to the contact pads. Try to cut away the bottom part as where that is where the wires will go through. I also leave the top part of the colored plastic housing to hide the connection and the diffusion from the side does a decent job of making it look natural so that it fits snug up against your sign's channel.
  4. Roughly cut the length of wire you will need to have to connect it to the next LED segment. I just run the wire through and along the channel in the back. It is very easy to hide extra wire in the channels and far more difficult if you make this too short so give yourself some extra room.
  5. Now just strip the wires, tin the ends and pads, and solder them to the LED contact pads. I try to get them to be fairly vertical to not create too many bends around the joint since they need to go through the sign. However, this is just dependent on sizing and where the pass through hole is located relative to the joint. Another tip to help this is to cut a little bit of the wire off that will be soldered on the bottom pad so the wires align better if they are connected.
  6. At this point I test that the connection is good by either multimeter or just touching them to some 12V lines to make sure it turns on. Completely optional but highly recommended is to now coat the joint in liquid electrical tape, hot glue also works fine but the cooling can snap the joint and not provide a good seal.
  7. Put that LED segment into place, feed the wire through the back and then continue to the next LED segment. Make sure to solder the next LED segment after it has been fed through the back. This is usually a more difficult solder since you dealing close the sign and on already connected wires. I recommend soldering the next section of wire to the LED before soldering the previous connection. Remember, test as you go along. It isn't the most fun thing to find out you have a bad joint halfway through when you are unsure which it could be.
  8. Repeat until all segments are connected. Go ahead and also solder in your DC cable if you haven't already.
  9. The last thing to do is to cable manage on the back of the sign. I just fit the connecting wires into the channel and hot glue them into place. I also place a glob on each hole where they pass through to better reinforce the joint from strain.
